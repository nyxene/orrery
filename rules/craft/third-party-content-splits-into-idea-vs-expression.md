---
rule: "third-party research content splits into freely-usable idea/technique and protected expression that needs permission"
scope: craft
evidence: "research passes over third-party course material surfaced generic technique and named-author-attributed text in the same source; set as policy before anything derived from it shipped"
---

When research surfaces content from a third-party source — a competitor's product, a course handbook, an article — always split findings into two buckets before recommending use: (1) freely usable general technique/ideas, standard terminology, and facts, including anything reached independently before seeing the source (corroboration is not the same as ownership); (2) content that needs explicit permission — verbatim text, a specific named author's proprietary formula or system, or wholesale reuse of another source's exact catalog/labels.

**Why:** Copyright covers substantial similarity, not just verbatim copying — rewording a source's sentence does not clear it into the freely-usable bucket. Implementing a well-known named technique from its general definition, in your own words/code, is normal industry practice; reproducing a specific author's exact formula or prose is a different risk category, regardless of how simple or old the underlying idea is. Conflating the two either blocks work that was always safe or ships work that carries real legal risk.

**How to apply:** When analyzing a new third-party source, explicitly split findings into the two buckets in the write-up, and flag the second bucket as needing a decision from whoever owns licensing/legal risk for the project — a coding agent is not qualified to rule on this and should present a risk breakdown, not a verdict. Within the "needs permission" bucket, distinguish "kept, gated on pursuing permission" (there's an actual path to the original author) from "parked" (no such path) — they have different next steps, don't collapse them into one "blocked" status. For content you do want to use, re-derive it from the underlying general idea independently; never clear it by editing the source's own sentence, however heavily.
